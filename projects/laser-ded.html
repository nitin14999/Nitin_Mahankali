Developing Laser DED process parameters for faster Ti-6Al-4V deposition

A full process-development chain, from single tracks to areas, volumes and tensile specimens, on a laser directed energy deposition machine with an inert-gas chamber. The work included DOE-based parameter development, CNC programming, solving a working-distance problem in tall builds, and comparing the mechanical properties of horizontal and vertical builds.

Objective

Laser DED builds metal parts by melting a powder stream with a laser, layer by layer. For Ti-6Al-4V, a higher deposition rate makes the process faster and more economical. It also changes how much heat goes into the part and how fast it cools, and that affects porosity, microstructure and strength.

The aim of the thesis was to increase the deposition rate without compromising material properties, and to understand which process parameters make that possible.

Role

Student researcher and Master's thesis candidate at Fraunhofer IWS, with full responsibility for the experimental work on the DED machine. This covered literature review, parameter selection, DOE planning, CNC programming, deposition of tracks, areas, volumes and test specimens, and their mechanical evaluation.

Thesis: M.Sc. Merge Technologies for Resource Efficiency, TU Chemnitz, carried out at Fraunhofer IWS.

Problem solving: losing the working distance in tall builds

Horizontal builds ran without problems. Tall vertical builds with a small footprint went wrong as they grew: the gap between the nozzle and the part kept shrinking, until the machine threw errors and the build had to stop.

Schematic of the problem and the fix. Not to scale.

ProblemIn tall walls with a small footprint, the gap between nozzle and part shrank layer by layer, until the machine stopped with errors. The walls also bulged at mid-height.
Root causeA small footprint means little conduction into the substrate, and it gets worse as the wall grows. Heat built up, so each layer came out higher than the programmed Z-step and the gap shrank. The trapped heat and constant laser input also caused thermal expansion and bulging at the mid-section.
SolutionCalculated the height gained per layer and how fast the working distance was shrinking. Then rewrote the G-code Z-increment so the laser focus and powder focus stayed on the part surface at a constant working distance.
ResultVertical builds were completed without errors and produced the specimens needed for tensile testing.

Today, closed-loop height control with sensor-based monitoring of melt-pool height would do this automatically. Calculating the height gain and correcting the program by hand reached the same result with the equipment available at the time.

Results: build orientation changes the properties

Horizontal builds

Higher tensile strength but lower ductility than standard Ti-6Al-4V.

Large footprint, fast heat dissipation into the substrate.

Vertical builds

Lower tensile strength than the horizontal builds but higher ductility, closer to standard Ti-6Al-4V.

Small footprint, heat accumulates as the wall grows.

Interpretation

The likely explanation is the thermal history. Horizontal builds cool quickly, which favours fine α′ martensite: high strength, low ductility. In vertical builds the trapped heat keeps reheating earlier layers. This in-situ (intrinsic) heat treatment can temper or decompose the martensite into a more ductile α + β structure, trading some strength for ductility.

This is a hypothesis based on the mechanical results and the thermal behaviour seen during building. It was not confirmed by microstructure analysis of the specimens, which would be the next step.

Key learnings

  • Thermal history matters as much as the process parameters: the same parameters behave differently in a large horizontal block and a thin vertical wall.
  • Geometry-dependent heat accumulation needs a process strategy, such as Z-step correction, interlayer dwell times or closed-loop height control.
  • A staged plan (tracks → areas → volumes → specimens) catches problems early and cheaply.
  • Writing machine code directly (G-code / M-code) makes it possible to fix process problems at the source.
  • Results from a different setup are worth taking as hypotheses and then checking with targeted analysis.
