An ammonia refuelling station for refrigerated and pressure-liquefied NH₃

The concept and engineering design of a refuelling station that stores and dispenses ammonia in both liquid forms: refrigerated at about −33 °C, and pressure-liquefied at ambient temperature. Boil-off gas is recovered, and ammonia can be transferred between the two forms on demand. Work covers PFDs and P&IDs, the transfer and boil-off logic, and the refuelling protocol.

Objective

If ammonia is going to power vehicles and vessels, it needs refuelling stations, much like petrol stations today. Ammonia is a gas at ambient conditions, so it has to be stored as a liquid in one of two ways: refrigerated below its boiling point of about −33 °C at atmospheric pressure, or pressure-liquefied at about 10 bar at ambient temperature.

The goal is a station concept that can store both forms, recover boil-off gas instead of venting it, switch between the forms on demand, and dispense whichever form the customer needs.

Role

Research Scientist responsible for the literature review, the process concept, the PFDs and P&IDs, the boil-off and transfer logic, and the refuelling protocol from storage tank to vehicle.

Status: concept and engineering design. The station has not been built yet, and a dedicated area of the test field has been allocated for a future implementation.

How the station works

Boil-off gas management

Recovering heat leak, not venting it

Even a well-insulated refrigerated tank takes in heat, so some ammonia evaporates as boil-off gas (BOG). Instead of being vented, the BOG is collected and compressed into the pressure-liquefied storage, where it is kept as liquid at ambient temperature.

Cold → pressurised

Serving pressure-liquefied demand

If a customer needs pressure-liquefied ammonia and only refrigerated stock is available, ammonia is drawn from the refrigerated tank and brought up to storage pressure in the pressure-liquefied tanks, ready to dispense.

Pressurised → cold

Flash cooling through an expansion valve

For refrigerated demand, pressure-liquefied ammonia is expanded through an expansion valve. In this adiabatic expansion part of the liquid flashes to vapour, which cools the rest towards −33 °C. A chiller or condenser in the refrigerated tank re-liquefies the flash vapour.

Dispensing

Two dispensers, one protocol

Each storage mode feeds its own dispenser. A refuelling protocol defines how ammonia is transferred from the station tanks to the vehicle, with connection, transfer and disconnection steps and the safety checks around them.

Why ammonia: a practical hydrogen carrier

Ammonia is about 17.8 wt % hydrogen and can be cracked back into hydrogen and nitrogen where hydrogen is needed. Compared with liquid hydrogen it is far easier to keep in liquid form:

Liquid NH₃ storage temperature≈ −33 °C
Liquid H₂ storage temperature≈ −253 °C
H₂ per m³: liquid NH₃≈ 121 kg
H₂ per m³: liquid H₂≈ 71 kg

Approximate literature values. Temperature bars show cooling below 0 °C. Liquid ammonia holds more hydrogen per cubic metre than liquid hydrogen itself.

Ammonia route

Store and transport ammonia at −33 °C or about 10 bar. Use it directly as a fuel, or crack it on site (NH₃ → N₂ + H₂) for hydrogen applications.

Liquid hydrogen route

Requires cryogenic storage at −253 °C, which means more energy for liquefaction, more complex insulation and higher boil-off losses.

Key learnings

  • Boil-off is unavoidable with refrigerated storage. Designing for BOG recovery from the start turns a loss and a safety risk into usable product.
  • Combining refrigerated and pressure-liquefied storage gives flexibility: bulk storage at low pressure, plus a pressurised buffer for customers and recovered BOG.
  • Thermodynamics drives the equipment choice: compression to move vapour up in pressure, adiabatic expansion and chilling to bring liquid down in temperature.
  • Ammonia's much milder storage conditions compared with liquid hydrogen are a key argument for ammonia as a hydrogen carrier.
