Objective

Ammonia is a carbon-free fuel that is easier to store and carry on board than hydrogen. But it is hard to burn in an engine: it ignites poorly, burns slowly and can form NOx from the nitrogen it contains. Adding hydrogen speeds up combustion, but too much hydrogen raises combustion temperatures and NOx.

The aim is to find out how the NH₃ / H₂ blend ratio and the operating conditions affect combustion, engine performance and emissions in an engine intended for an ammonia-fuelled yacht.

Role

Research Scientist on the engine test bench. Responsibilities: literature review of NH₃ and H₂ as engine fuels, the controlled combustion experiments on the dynamometer, cylinder-pressure and exhaust-gas measurements, and a Python-based workflow for processing and analysing the combustion data.

Experiments are running. Quantitative results will be added once they can be published.

Ammonia: benefits and drawbacks

+ carbon-free, high energy density as a liquid, established handling, high knock resistance.

− poor ignition and slow burning, which limits combustion stability. Nitrogen in the fuel can form NOx, unburned NH₃ can pass into the exhaust (NH₃ slip), and it is toxic.

Hydrogen: benefits and drawbacks

+ fast, easily ignited combustion that strengthens and stabilises the NH₃ flame.

− at high H₂ fractions, higher combustion temperatures increase thermal NOx. The literature review identified this as a key trade-off, along with the difficulty of storing H₂ on board.

From pressure trace to heat release

Cylinder pressure is recorded against crank angle, then processed in Python (NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib). The apparent heat-release rate comes from the first law of thermodynamics:

dQ/dθ = γ/(γ−1) · p · dV/dθ + 1/(γ−1) · V · dp/dθ

From this the workflow evaluates heat-release behaviour, combustion phasing and stability, and correlates them with fuel composition, operating point, torque and exhaust emissions.

Scope of work

  • Literature review of ammonia and hydrogen as engine fuels: combustion properties, benefits, drawbacks and emission pathways.
  • Controlled combustion experiments with NH₃ / H₂ blends on the engine–dynamometer test bench.
  • Crank-angle-resolved in-cylinder pressure measurement and data acquisition.
  • Exhaust-gas analysis with a focus on NOx and unburned ammonia.
  • Python workflows for data processing, heat-release analysis and visualisation.
  • Correlation of fuel composition and operating conditions with torque and combustion behaviour.

Key learnings so far

  • Ammonia's weakness, slow and hard-to-ignite combustion, can be offset with hydrogen, but only up to a point.
  • The H₂ fraction is a trade-off between combustion stability and NOx, so the "best" blend depends on the operating point.
  • Cylinder pressure against crank angle is the most direct window into combustion, and a solid analysis workflow turns raw traces into comparable metrics.
  • Emissions from ammonia combustion (NOx, NH₃ slip) need to be considered from the start, especially for marine use.

Next steps

Complete the test matrix, map combustion and emissions across blend ratios and operating points, and identify a recommended operating window for the yacht engine.
