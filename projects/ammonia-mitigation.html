Ammonia release mitigation for an ammonia-fuelled yacht

A two-stage capture system that keeps leaked ammonia on board instead of releasing it to people or the water. A water scrubbing tank provides the bulk capture, and a membrane contactor with sulfuric acid treats the rest. Work covers the process concept, the design of two dedicated test rigs, and parametric studies of both capture stages.

Objective

Ammonia is a promising carbon-free marine fuel, but it isn't harmless like diesel or gasoline when it escapes. It's toxic to the people on board, and released over water it's harmful to aquatic life. On a yacht the fuel is stored in a pressurised tank, so even a small leak has to be contained.

The goal is a mitigation system that diverts any leaked ammonia from the fuel-tank enclosure and removes at least 99 % of it before the remaining gas is vented.

Role

Research Scientist responsible for the process concept, the PFD/P&ID and equipment selection, the instrumentation concept, and the design and planning of the experimental programme for both capture stages.

Work in progress. The test rigs and parametric campaigns are under way, and results will be added once they are available.

Why two stages

Stage 1 · Water scrubbing

Ammonia is very soluble in water (NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻), which makes water a cheap, high-capacity capture medium. Recirculated water is sprayed from the top of the tank by a centrifugal pump with a variable-frequency drive (VFD), increasing the gas–liquid contact area.

Limitation: in a fast release the gas flow is high and the residence time in the tank is short, so some ammonia passes through. Uptake also drops as the water loads with ammonia.

Stage 2 · Membrane contactor

The residual gas passes a hydrophobic membrane, with sulfuric acid circulating on the other side. Ammonia diffuses through the gas-filled pores and reacts irreversibly (2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄). This keeps its liquid-side partial pressure near zero and the driving force high.

Design constraint: the transmembrane pressure has to stay below the membrane's liquid entry pressure to avoid pore wetting.

Experimental programme: two rigs, studied separately

Each capture stage is characterised on its own rig, so its effects can be isolated before the two are combined. On both rigs, NH₃ concentration is measured at the inlet and outlet, and the main response is the removal efficiency:

η = (Cin − Cout) / Cin × 100 %

Rig 1 · Water scrubbing tank

Compressed air (simulating the enclosure atmosphere) is dosed with NH₃ through mass flow controllers, mixed in a static mixer, and fed to the tank.

Key learnings so far

  • Ammonia safety is a design driver, not an add-on: marine use needs containment and treatment, not just detection.
  • Physical absorption in water and chemical absorption in acid complement each other: one gives cheap capacity, the other a high driving force for the last few percent.
  • Characterising each stage separately is what makes the results useful for scale-up.
  • Measuring NH₃ reliably is a challenge in its own right: analyser choice, calibration gases and material compatibility decide data quality.
  • In membrane contactors the pressure balance is as important as the flow rates, because a wetted membrane loses most of its performance.

Next steps

Run both parametric campaigns, model the capture efficiency of each stage, then size the integrated two-stage system for realistic leak scenarios on board.
