Making a solid oxide fuel cell system smaller, cleaner and ready for ammonia

Development work on a portable SOFC power system: a redesigned propane start-up burner with complete combustion, a larger and more effective heat exchanger, a one-piece additively manufactured burner/heat-exchanger block built into the base plate, and the root-cause analysis of interconnect damage during ammonia operation.

Objective

A portable SOFC system weighs about 60–70 kg. Most of the volume is ceramic insulation, but the metal parts around it (burner, heat exchanger, piping, base plate) leave a lot of unused space and need many assembly steps.

The goals were to reduce volume, weight and the number of assembly parts, to bring the start-up burner's CO emissions within limits, and to evaluate ammonia as an alternative fuel.

Role

Development Engineer on the SOFC system team, responsible for the start-up burner and heat-exchanger design, its redesign for additive manufacturing, and testing on propane and ammonia, including exhaust analysis and troubleshooting.

Design for additive manufacturing: from an assembly to one block

Concept schematic. The actual geometry belongs to the company and isn't shown.

ProblemThe propane start-up burner produced CO above the permitted limits, and part of its heat left with the exhaust instead of warming the system.
Root causeIncomplete combustion in the burner, and a heat-exchanger area too small to recover the heat.
SolutionRedesigned the burner for complete combustion, with fine pores to prevent flashback. Enlarged the heat-transfer area and integrated both into one block designed for additive manufacturing.
ResultPropane burned completely and CO emissions came within limits. More heat stayed in the system, and fewer parts were needed to assemble it.

Ammonia operation: finding why the interconnects failed

The first ammonia tests ran well. After continued operation, however, the metallic interconnects were damaged. They're the parts that carry current between the cells, and the most critical component after the cells themselves.

ProblemInterconnect material degraded once ammonia was fed directly to the fuel-cell modules.
Root causeAt SOFC operating temperatures, ammonia attacks the metallic interconnects (for example through nitridation) and causes corrosive wear. The investigation compared operation with and without upstream cracking, varied air and fuel flow, and studied the interconnect materials.
SolutionCrack the ammonia into hydrogen and nitrogen before it enters the stack, so the interconnects never see raw NH₃ at high temperature.
ResultA practical route to running SOFC modules on ammonia, supported by a comparison of electrical output on ammonia and on propane.

Burner and fuel

Key learnings

  • Additive manufacturing pays off most when the design is rethought, not just copied: part consolidation and better use of space beat printing the old parts.
  • Combustion quality and heat recovery have to be designed together in a compact system.
  • A new fuel brings new failure modes: ammonia is a hydrogen carrier, but it attacks materials at high temperature, so conditioning the fuel can matter as much as the stack itself.
  • Root-cause analysis needs both operating data and material knowledge.
